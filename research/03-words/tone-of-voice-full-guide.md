# UBS Tone of Voice — Complete Guide

> Extracted from the official UBS document: `Tone_of_voice_en_150318.pdf` (18 March 2015).
> All examples are genuine pieces of UBS writing. The rewrites are suggestions checked by Legal and Compliance.

---

## Brand Personality

We want people to feel we're:

- **Optimistic by nature**
- **Purposefully intelligent**
- **Confidently human**

That's our brand personality. As writers, our job is to bring it to life by always thinking from our reader's point of view.

---

## The Three Pillars

| Pillar | Reader thinks | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **Clear** | "I understand it" | Simple, concise, understandable. Easy to understand first time. |
| **Convincing** | "I believe it" | Relevant, believable, confident. In control of our subject. In tune with what readers are interested in. |
| **...with Charm** | "I like it" | Readable, likeable, memorable. Distinctive through personality. We keep readers reading (not just because they have to). |

**The chain:** Good writing → Good marketing and communications → Differentiating.

---

## In a Nutshell

### Clear — "I understand it"
- **Keep it simple:** Five words instead of ten
- **Put the main point first:** Start with what matters most
- **Use subheadings and bullet points:** Structured, easy to skim
- **Write like you speak:** Use everyday words and phrases
- **Use active verbs:** Clearer and more human

### Convincing — "I believe it"
- **Only say what you need to:** Focus on the essentials
- **Benefits before processes:** What's important for readers, not how we got there
- **Concrete not abstract:** Say what you mean, avoid jargon
- **Evidence not adjectives:** Use facts to make readers believe
- **Only claim what you can prove:** Make Legal and Compliance happy too

### ...with Charm — "I like it"
- **Be personal:** Talk about we, us and you
- **Vary the rhythm:** Mix long and short sentences
- **Make observations that resonate:** Include truths everyone can relate to
- **Ask questions (including "what if?"):** Draw readers in
- **Paint pictures:** Help them imagine what you're describing
- **Use the occasional aside:** (But with a light touch.)

## Clear — In Detail

### 1. Keep it simple (1/7)

If you're using a few sentences to say just one thing, there's probably a simpler way. Imagine you're explaining it to someone, and start with "which basically means that...". Now try writing it like that.

**Before:**
> Tax information is provided based upon our understanding of current tax legislation and applicable rates. These may change in the future and they may not apply to your individual circumstances. Please be aware that UBS does not give tax advice and you should therefore consult your own legal and tax advisors, as you deem necessary, before entering into any investment.

**After:**
> Remember to check whether investments are suitable from a tax point of view. We've given you information based on current laws and rates, but we can't advise you on this. So talk to your legal or tax advisor to make sure.

### 2. Put the main point first (2/7)

What does your reader need to know, feel or do? Think about it from their point of view, not just ours. Then start with the most important thing. Don't bury it halfway down.

**Try this:** Cut your first sentence. Does what you've written still work? If it does, you've taken too long to get to the point.

Think about what the reader needs to:
- **Know** — My fees are changing
- **Feel** — Everything's under control
- **Do** — Get in touch now

**Before:**
> Dear Mr. Smith, As of 1 January 2014, the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) strictly prohibits the promotion of Non Mainstream Pooled Investments (NMPI) to retail investors, unless an exception applies.

**After:**
> Dear Mr. Smith, If you'd like to invest in Non-Mainstream Pooled Investments, please sign the form we've sent you to confirm you understand the features and risks. This is because, from January 2014, it will be against Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) rules to promote Non-Mainstream Pooled Investments (NMPI) to retail investors.

### 3. Avoid long sentences or paragraphs (3/7)

**Rule: Keep sentences under 30 words** (but don't always hit that max). Stick to one topic per paragraph.

**Before (51 words, one sentence):**
> In the event that you have a complaint concerning harassment, wish to report an incident or learn that conduct of the nature prohibited by the UBS Anti-Harassment and Non-Discrimination policy may be occurring, you must immediately report the matter to your supervisor, your next level manager or a Human Resources Advisor.

**After (two sentences: 16 words, then 19 words):**
> If you're being harassed, report it to your supervisor, next level manager or HR right away. You should also do this if you find out that someone isn't complying with our 'Anti-harassment and non-discrimination policy'.

### 4. Use subheadings and bullet points (4/7)

A clear structure makes it easy for your reader to find their way around. Use subheadings as signposts and help them skim read.

**Try this:** Hold the piece of writing out in front of you, so you can't read the body copy. Is the structure still clear from the subheadings?

**Three types of subheadings** (don't mix them in the same piece):
- **Labels:** Our view in short
- **Summaries:** We think equities will outperform bonds
- **Attention-grabbers:** Feeling cautious?

**Bullet point rules:**
- Use a consistent style (all verbs, all questions, or all full sentences, but don't mix)
- No more than five per group; if more, split under separate headings
- Full sentences: start with capital, end with period
- Fragments: start with lower case

**Before:**
> The Bahamas, historically enjoying political stability, offer a well-developed and sophisticated banking and trust environment with more than 250 well-respected and licensed banks and trust companies. Its excellent location, only 185 miles from Miami and sharing the same time zone as the New York Stock Exchange and many Latin American countries, situates well for any international investment business.

**After:**
> The Bahamas have a sophisticated banking and trust industry. They're also:
> - politically stable
> - home to more than 250 licensed (and respected) banks and trust companies
> - just 185 miles from Miami
> - in the same time zone as the New York Stock Exchange

### 5. Use everyday words and phrases (6/7)

Use words you'd say face-to-face. Formal language makes things complex and puts distance between you and your reader. You won't be dumbing down your message. You'll sound smarter by showing you're in control of your subject.

**Try this:** Read what you've written out loud. Does it sound like how you'd say it? If it doesn't, the writing's too formal. If you're running out of breath, the sentences are too long. If you're stumbling, so will your reader.

#### Word Replacement Table

| Instead of | Try |
|---|---|
| Additional | Extra / more |
| Allow | Let |
| Assist | Help |
| Disregard | Ignore |
| Due to | Because |
| Ensure | Make sure |
| Following | After |
| However | But |
| Improved | Better |
| In order to | To |
| Please note | Remember |
| Purchase | Buy |
| Prior to | Before |
| Provide | Give |
| Regarding | About |
| Require | Need |
| Such as | Including / like |
| Therefore | So |
| Utilise | Use |

**Before:**
> As a consequence of your classification as an Elective Professional you will lose the specific protections afforded to Private Clients in respect of NMPIs.

**After:**
> Because you're now classified as an Elective Professional, when it comes to NMPIs you won't get the same protection as Private Clients.

### 6. Use active verbs (7/7)

Passive verbs make us sound cold and bureaucratic and hide who's doing something.

**Passive:** The rates will be calculated on an annual basis.
**Active:** We'll calculate the rates each year.

**Passive (avoiding responsibility):** Mistakes were made.
**Active (owning it):** We made a mistake.

**Contractions** make writing sound more everyday. So "we will", "we have" and "you will" become "we'll", "we've" and "you'll". But take care: some contractions sound strange (it'd, what'll, could've).

## Convincing — In Detail

### 1. Only say what you need to (1/5)

What's your main message? Everything you write should help get it across. If it doesn't, cut it.

**Example:** A client letter for someone who's already signed up for UBS Property Financing doesn't need paragraphs about how the product was "designed specifically for our clients" or is "exclusively available in the UK". The client already knows. Cut it.

### 2. Benefits before processes (2/5)

What does your reader care about? What's in it for them? Start with that.

**The two-step process:**
1. Think of something we do (e.g. "provide accurate reporting"). What does it mean for clients? Start with the functional benefit: **Reliable information.**
2. Ask what that means for clients to find the emotional benefit: **Being able to make investment decisions that feel right.**

**Try this:** Ask "so what?" and keep asking it until you get to the benefit.

**Before:**
> We provide accurate reporting.

**After:**
> You'll be able to make decisions that feel right, because our reporting is accurate.

### 3. Concrete, not abstract (3/5)

Concrete phrases mean more than abstract ideas. Instead of "integrated bank" say "all our teams work together".

Jargon or technical language can sound abstract. If you really can't avoid it, explain it.

**Before:**
> You receive personalized and adjustable portfolio proposals. Our open architecture approach means you will be offered the most suitable instruments and products to achieve your goals, not only from UBS, but also from other carefully selected top providers.

**After:**
> You'll get personal portfolio proposals that can change with your needs. You'll be able to choose products from us and other providers who we hand-pick.

### 4. Evidence, not adjectives (4/5)

Which is more believable: "We offer a responsive service" or "We answer your calls 24 hours a day, every day"?

Give readers evidence rather than unsupported adjectives. Readers need a reason to believe adjectives like: transparent, robust, comprehensive, helpful, easy.

**Try this:** Use the **as-opposed-to test**. Every time you find an adjective, imagine your reader saying "as opposed to what?" If the answer is ridiculous, you can do without the adjective.

**Example of empty adjectives:**
> At UBS, our response to the challenges our clients face is to offer a unique new investment process that builds on and extends our impressive existing capabilities.

What makes it unique? That's what would stick. What makes capabilities impressive? Give an example. Readers will make up their own minds.

### 5. Only claim what you can prove (5/5)

If you are going to use an adjective, make sure it's accurate.

#### Dangerous Adjectives (expect a call from Legal and Compliance)

- global
- leading
- perfect
- superior
- total
- unique

Be careful about promising things that aren't definite. It might be better to say we **can** do something, rather than we **will**, when you're talking about something we can't guarantee.

## ...with Charm — In Detail

### See the difference

Three versions of the same content show how charm transforms writing:

**Original (formal):**
> Your client advisor will use a structured advisory approach to understand your situation, find the appropriate investment solution based on your personal preferences, and determine how to make best use of our offering.

**Clear and convincing:**
> Your client advisor will get to know your aims. Then they'll find the right investments for you and make sure you make the most of what we can offer.

**With charm:**
> What's in it for you? A client advisor who'll get to know you, to understand where you are now and where you want to be. They'll pick the investments to take you there. And work out what else we can do for you. (So you don't miss out.)

### The Two Main Techniques (use all the time)

#### 1. Use personal pronouns

Use **we**, **us** and **you**. Sometimes we say "UBS" or "the bank" and talk about "clients". Personal pronouns sound more human. Imagine a real person when you're writing, a friend or a relative, rather than your boss.

#### 2. Vary the rhythm

Vary sentence length. Lots of short sentences sound robotic. Only long sentences are monotonous. Mix short and longer sentences (still **20-30 words max**). Use the occasional very short one to inject rhythm.

### Additional Charm Techniques (use when you can)

#### Be memorable

**Make observations that resonate:** Think about the bigger picture and how things fit into your reader's life.

**Before:**
> UBS offers innovative programs that enable employees to structure their working hours according to their individual needs and taking into account operational requirements.

**After:**
> Nine to five's not for everyone. If your job doesn't have to fit in those hours, we can talk about a different working day for you.

**Paint pictures:** Help your reader imagine what you're writing about.

**Before:**
> Our interactive tools enable short-, medium- and long-term analysis of movements in everything from currencies and stocks to global indices.

**After:**
> Use our online tools to analyze how the markets are doing. From your desk, from the taxi (or your garden).

**Use metaphors:** Familiar things to explain technical points or make a point vivid.

> In the war against the recession, the European Central Bank has hardly been the boldest of generals.

#### Be intriguing

**Ask questions:** Questions draw readers in. They also work well as subheadings.

**Before:**
> As a UBS client, you can subscribe to receive the CIO Monthly Letter on the CIO Portal of UBS e-banking or via your client advisor.

**After:**
> Want to keep up to speed with the markets? Use the CIO Portal or ask your client advisor about getting our monthly letter.

**Ask "what if?":**

**Before:**
> While many programs charge you 90,000 points for an airline ticket worth up to $900, with My Choice Rewards that same ticket is only 50,000 points, saving you up to 40,000 points.

**After:**
> What if you could fly away sooner, rather than later? With My Choice Rewards a $900 airline ticket is just 50,000 points, compared to the 90,000 that other credit card reward programs charge.

#### Use the occasional aside

An aside is a gentle wit, a disarming way of showing intelligence with a light touch. A few words in brackets to show readers we understand what life is like.

**Rule: No more than one or two in every 400 words.** You'll probably only use one in any single piece of writing.

**Example:**
> You decide whether members of your family or a professional will manage the fund. And they'll base their investment decisions on guidelines you've agreed. (We'll leave that part to you.)

### The Charm Dial

How much charm depends on **why** you're writing and **who for**.

**Dial up the charm when you're trying to persuade:**
- Bids, advertising, opinion pieces
- Marketing materials including brochures, invitations
- Internal communications like emails, intranet articles, announcements
- Use all the charm techniques, but only when it feels right

**Restrict yourself to personal pronouns and energy when you're explaining and informing:**
- Announcements and status letters
- Internal communications on sensitive subjects

## Headlines

Headlines are the first step to grabbing readers' attention. In the UBS brand design, they matter even more.

### The Two-Step Formula

1. **Keyline** — grab the reader's attention
2. **Infoline** — give a little more information

The two work together. The first sets up expectations and the second brings them into focus.

### Tone by element

- **Keyline:** Charm dominates
- **Infoline:** Mostly clear and convincing (though an aside can work)

### How to use our tone of voice in headlines

**Using charm:**
- Be personal: "we" and "our" works better than "UBS"
- Be intriguing: one-word keylines make readers curious; ask a question
- Be memorable: paint a picture of the value; tap into universal truths

**Being clear:**
- Keep it simple: use verbs instead of nouns, keep verbs active
- Use everyday words: formal words are never helpful, especially not in headlines

**Being convincing:**
- Benefits, not processes: focus on value, not product names
- Evidence, not adjectives: a fact has more impact

### Punctuation rules

- Keylines usually don't need punctuation, except question marks
- **Exception:** One-word keylines can use a period for emphasis: "Definitive."
- If the infoline has more than one sentence, end each with a period

### Examples

**Before:**
> Wealth Management UK — Protecting and managing wealth is at the heart of what we do

**After:**
> Green fingers — Protecting your wealth and helping it grow. It's what we do.

**Before:**
> UBS Investment Bank — Being client-centric is in our DNA

**After:**
> Of course — Investment banking that puts you first.

**Before:**
> UBS Investment Mandates — All of our financial competence in one optimized solution for you

**After:**
> Together — Our investment know-how, your way.

## Bringing It All Together — Before/After Examples

### Asset Management: Strategic Investment Advisory

**Before:**
> Strategic Investment Advisory — Within our Global Investment Solutions group we have recently created a distinct unit called Strategic Investment Advisory (SIA). SIA provides clients with a range of services including investment policy development, total portfolio construction and management incorporating alternative assets, risk management services, global tactical asset allocation, and multi-manager research and portfolio construction. Additionally, SIA provides Asset Liability Management and Fiduciary Management for pension funds. SIA draws upon a broad range of professional experience in the SIA team and beyond, including former consultants and actuaries, former pension plan sponsors or trustees, research analysts, risk managers, derivatives specialists and individuals from a variety of other professional and academic backgrounds. We provide advice to a range of institutional clients including pension funds, endowments, insurance companies, friendly societies, charities and family offices.

**After:**
> Strategic investment advice — Investment specialists need specialist advice. We have a team to make sure you get it. We help you develop investment policy, build your portfolio and manage it. We've put the team together to advise clients from pension funds, endowments and insurance companies to friendly societies, charities and family offices. The service includes:
> - alternative assets
> - risk management
> - tactical asset allocation
> - multi-manager portfolios.
>
> And for pension funds, the team offers asset liability management and fiduciary management. We call it Strategic Investment Advisory (SIA). Many specialties make for deeper insights. So the team includes former consultants and actuaries, pension plan sponsors and trustees, research managers and derivatives specialists. (Not to mention others from beyond finance.)

**What changed:**
- **Clear:** Main point first; bullet points; removed formal words (within, provides, draws upon, additionally, a variety of); longest sentence now 25 words (was 43)
- **Convincing:** Opening emphasises what clients get, not internal team structure
- **Charm:** Opening observation is memorable; more use of "we"; varied sentence lengths; team diversity turned into an aside

## Checklist

A step-by-step guide to being clear and convincing, and adding charm when you need to.

### Planning

1. **Who's the audience?** The first step to working out what to include and how much charm to use. What would they want to read?
2. **What's the main point?** Write that down — you've got your first sentence.
3. **What questions would your audience have about that main point?** Use those to come up with subheadings. Put them in order of priority.
4. **Do you have the content you need?** Make sure you've got the facts that will answer your readers' questions and evidence to back up any claims.

**Extra tip:** Double-check your plan. Do you need all the things you've listed? Maybe some at the bottom aren't really necessary.

### Writing

5. **Are you writing about benefits instead of processes?** Try to begin your sentences with "You" as much as possible to start with. It will help you think along the right lines.
6. **Do you really need every adjective?** Have you got any evidence? Can you write about that to show what you mean?
7. **Are your sentences short enough?** Try writing in short sentences, then join some together. That's easier than untangling long ones. **Remember 20-30 words max.** Mix them up.

**Extra tip:** You might not notice you've used jargon or formal words as you're going along. So read with that in mind. Change any you spot.

### Polishing

8. **Is everything you've said getting the main point across?** Test every paragraph and be brave — cut anything that doesn't help your reader get the message.
9. **Does it sound human?** Not chatty or casual. Just how you would if you were talking to your reader one-to-one.
10. **Have you used the right amount of charm?** Look back at the brief. If you're aiming to get a reaction, look for opportunities to ask questions, paint pictures or add an aside. If you're writing about something very serious or sensitive — bad news for example — think again about whether you're being too lighthearted.

**Extra tip:** Does your audience speak English as a first language? Will they understand all the phrases you've used? If in doubt, ask someone to check.

## Briefing Writers

Good writing starts with a good brief. Without it, the writer is working in the dark.

### The basics — tell them:

- **What kind of writing it is:** Email, letter, webpage, brochure, ad? Do you know the word count?
- **Who the reader is:** Including how much they know about the topic and if English is their first language
- **What they need to know, feel and do:** And which is most important
- **Where the content is coming from:** Researching independently? Interviewing people? Analysing data?
- **What we want to achieve:** Persuading people? Making sure they know about a regulation?

### The practicalities:

- **How the writing relates to anything else the reader has seen from us:** Do writers need to refer to something or follow a specific format?
- **The timetable:** When do you need the first draft? How much time for feedback and rewriting?

### Our tone of voice:

**For in-house writers:**
- Check they know about it and how to use it, especially charm
- Suggest they use the checklist
- Tell them about training

**For external writers:**
- Introduce the tone of voice using "In short"
- Make sure they have the full guidelines
- Show them the section about adding more charm if the work calls for it
- Tell them about the checklist

## Giving Feedback

The question isn't "do you like it?" It's:
- Does it follow the brief?
- Is it clear and convincing, with charm?

### Step one: Check the brief

That tells you:
- What kind of writing it is
- Who the reader is
- What they need to know, feel and do
- What we're trying to achieve
- How the writing relates to anything else the reader has seen from us

### Step two: Break feedback into three categories

#### Content — what we're saying
- Is all the key information there?
- Is it relevant to the audience?
- Is it clear what we want readers to know, feel and do?
- Are the facts right?
- Is the information specific enough?
- Does it make the benefits clear (not just the features and process)?
- Is there jargon? Do we need to explain it?
- If we make any claims, can we back them up with evidence?

#### Structure — the order we're saying it in
- Is the main point first? Do we get to it quickly?
- Is it broken up with enough subheadings?
- Are there any sentences over 30 words, or paragraphs with more than one topic?

#### Tone — how we're saying it
- Does it use everyday words and phrases?
- Are the sentences mostly the same length? If they are, there's not enough energy.
- Is our opinion clear? Do we get it across confidently?
- Do we paint a picture, so it's easy to imagine what we're describing?
- Would an aside liven it up? Or are there too many?

## Quick Reference — Dangerous Adjectives

Never use without proof: **global, leading, perfect, superior, total, unique**

Also suspect: **transparent, robust, comprehensive, helpful, easy, innovative, state-of-the-art, outstanding, superb, market-leading**
